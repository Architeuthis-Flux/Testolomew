# Wireless probe position sensing for Jumperless V6

Everything worked out so far about how a cable-free Jumperless probe could be located on the board: the survey of sensing methods, the numbers behind each, the two approaches picked to prototype, and what was learned setting up the magnetometer proof of concept on the CH32H417.

Compiled 2026-09-17 from the claude.ai "V6" project chat *Wireless active probe sensing for breadboard connections* (2026-08-30, exported in `V6pile/claude-chats/05-…md`) and from the work of bringing up Testolomew. Where this document corrects something said in that chat, section 11 lists it.

**Status in one paragraph.** Two approaches survived the survey. (1) *Capacitive ratio ladder*: the probe drives a ~50 kHz square wave from its tip, the 92-resistor ladder already under the V5 probe pads acts as a current divider, and the board reads the carrier at both ladder ends; position is a ratio, so grip and hover height cancel. It gives the position along the pad strip, to about a millimetre, for almost no hardware. A proof-of-concept spec for V5 hardware exists (`ratio-ladder-poc-spec.md`, next to this file) and has not been run yet. (2) *Magnet plus 3-axis magnetometer array*: a small magnet in the probe, a sparse grid of TMAG5273 Hall sensors under the board, and a dipole fit that returns x, y, height and tilt. It is the only candidate that gives full 3D position with a passive probe. A 4×2 array is wired to a nanoCH32H417 and the firmware in this repository reads it, fits it and draws it; it runs on the board with all eight sensors, the array has been self-calibrated from a recording, and a first ruler test gives 10 mm steps to about ±0.5 mm at 30 mm height with a small magnet (§6.1).

---

## 1. What is wanted

The V5 probe is wired: a TRRRS cable carries the needle's voltage back, and the board works out which pad it touches from a 92-resistor divider. V6 wants the cable gone, and wants more than "which row":

| Need | Accuracy | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Which row | ±1.2 mm along the board | today's behaviour |
| Which hole | ±0.8 mm in both axes | select a–e within a row |
| A leg of a SOT-23 on a breakout | ±0.2 mm | stretch goal |
| Hover and height | a few mm | preview before touching, gestures |
| Tilt | ~10° | find the tip when the sensed point is up the barrel |

Constraints: the probe is battery powered (an nRF-class microcontroller was assumed), the board has a second processor to spend on this (now the CH32H417, see `V6pile/jumperless-v6-design-notes.md` §1), the user's circuit on the breadboard must not be disturbed and must not disturb the measurement, and cost matters at the dollar level.

## 2. How an Apple Pencil works, and the one idea worth stealing

It is an active electrostatic stylus. The iPad's touch grid is the receiver and the Pencil is the transmitter: the tip drives a modulated signal into the grid of electrodes (~4–5 mm pitch), each electrode receives an amplitude that falls with distance, and the position is a centroid interpolated across several electrodes, far finer than the pitch. A second electrode, a ring a little way up the tip, gives a second centroid; the offset between the two is the tilt. Pressure is measured in the pen and sent over Bluetooth, which is also how the iPad tells pen from palm. Newer iPads pick the tip up about a centimetre above the glass, which is hover.

**The idea worth stealing: the Pencil has no ground wire.** Its ground is the hand holding it (~100 pF to the body, the body ~100–200 pF to earth) plus a few pF of the pen's own capacitance to the room, and earth closes the loop back to the tablet. At hundreds of kHz that capacitance is a few kΩ, which is nothing next to a megohm input. So the wireless version of any contact scheme is "make the signal AC and let the hand be the return". The original Jumperless probe already did the transmit half (it scanned rows for a PWM signal from the probe); it only used a wire for the return.

## 3. The survey

Each method below has the physics that decides it and the number that limits it. Section 4 compares them.

### 3.1 Conductive pads without the wire

**Beacon pads (board transmits, probe receives).** Every pad is driven with its own digital pattern (time-sliced "I am pad 37" at ~200 kHz, whole board in ~10 ms, or CDMA). The needle is a high-impedance input into a comparator or timer capture; on contact it sees a full-swing signal, decodes the ID locally, and only reports the result over radio. With a gain stage it hears a pad from a couple of mm away, and comparing two neighbours' loudness interpolates between them. While touching, the probe can also answer back through the same contact. The catch is ~100 driven outputs: twelve shift registers. Rejected as inelegant.

**Ratiometric ladder, board drives (the fix for the 100 drivers).** What is needed is 100 distinguishable voltages, not 100 drivers, and the resistor ladder already makes them. Drive end A with a square wave while end B is grounded (pad k sits at V·k/N), then swap (V·(N−k)/N). The probe measures its received amplitude in each slot and computes k = N·A/(A+B). Everything unknown (grip, body capacitance, pad source impedance, input capacitance) multiplies both slots equally and cancels. Two GPIOs, nothing else. ~300 fixes/s. Hovering over the strip reads a weighted average of several pads, which is a continuous position.

**Same, with two PWMs from one timer.** Drive end A at 25 % duty and end B at 75 % from the same counter. Every pad then carries the same three-level staircase and only the middle level moves with position; the two step heights are A's and B's contributions and they sum to V whatever the coupling. Reference and measurement land inside one period. Two probe-side details: measure the *edges*, not the plateaus (the probe is AC-coupled through the body, a high-pass with τ = R_in × ~100 pF, so plateaus droop), and keep both outputs on one counter so the edges align. Two different *frequencies* also works (two Goertzel bins) but the capacitive return scales with 1/f, so the tones see slightly different paths.

**Ratio ladder, probe drives (the one chosen, §5).** Reverse the roles: the probe's tip injects a carrier capacitively wherever it is along the strip, the ladder splits that current toward its two ends in inverse proportion to resistance, and the board reads both ends. The probe side collapses to one toggling GPIO.

**Superposition coding.** Each pad gets two resistors, to a row line and a column line; 10 + 10 outputs cover 100 pads. **WS2811 chips on the LED data line** as pad drivers: reuses infrastructure, still 34 chips.

### 3.2 Getting to hole level: something on the breadboard face

- **2D resistive sheet.** One printed carbon-ink sheet with holes punched, four edge electrodes, X gradient in one period and Y in the next, probe reads X = A/(A+B), Y = C/(C+D). A 4-wire resistive touchscreen where the needle is the second layer. Four pins, one printed part, ~0.3 mm after a per-hole calibration table (the holes warp the field; the LEDs can run the calibration as "tap the lit hole"). Contact or near-contact only, no tilt. All four edges cannot be driven in one period: the top/bottom electrodes would short the left–right gradient.
- **Beacon grid flex.** 10 column strips and 60 row strips on a flex laminated to the face, all transmitting codes; the tip in a hole hears its row and column loudest and neighbours' ratios interpolate. A real digitizer with the analog in the probe, isolated from user circuits, hover works. ~70 drivers again.
- **Off-the-shelf mutual-cap touch controller** with active-pen support on the same flex: most capable, most vendor lock-in.
- **Column strips plus the existing crossbar, passive probe.** Inject a small AC burst into row N through the crossbar; ten face strips receive and the one nearest the metal tip wins. Row × column with a plain needle and no battery, but it puts a signal into a live user circuit.

### 3.3 Light

**Coded LEDs with a photodetector in the probe.** If there is an LED under every hole, modulate each with its ID on top of whatever it is showing. WS2812B-V6 sets the limits: 800 kbit/s data (30 µs per LED, so 500 LEDs on one chain is 15 ms), a >280 µs reset, and a 2 kHz internal PWM that chops any level from 1 to 254. Thirty-two parallel chains from PIO reach ~1.3 kHz frames, and the reset floor caps it at 2–3 kHz whatever the chain count. Bits must span several PWM cycles, so frames run at ≤250–500 Hz. Encode as Manchester on a 5–10 % brightness delta (invisible above 250 Hz, mean brightness unchanged, the probe only needs the sign of the change) with Gray-coded IDs so neighbours differ by one bit. Realistic: 9-bit ID in ~100 ms, ~10 fixes/s, or ~25/s if the board sends its frame clock over BLE. Better split: pads or ladder give the row, LEDs only resolve a–e (3 bits, ~30 ms). Two escapes: toggling a channel between 0 and 1 emits an invisible 2 µs full-current pulse every 500 µs that a fast photodiode sees, but only on otherwise-dark LEDs; and APA102/SK9822-class SPI LEDs (10+ MHz clock, ~19 kHz PWM) remove the limit if they exist small enough. WS2812s cannot be run backwards as photodiodes; plain matrix-driven LEDs could.

**VEML3328 colour sensor (LCSC C509073, ~$0.59).** RGB + clear + IR, 16-bit, 2×1.25 mm, but its integration times are 50/100/200/400 ms, so 20 Hz at best. It cannot read temporal codes; the scheme would become "each hole glows its ID" as chromaticity (30–60 separable colours with per-LED calibration, limited by light mixing from the neighbouring hole), row frame then column frame, a fix every ~200 ms, and it fights with what the LEDs are meant to show. Wrong part. What fixes it, from the same LCSC category: a bare phototransistor or PIN diode ($0.03–0.15) plus one op-amp, the cheapest and fastest option there is; or a fast colour sensor (TCS3472/TCS3400, 2.4 ms integration, $1–2). TSOP-style IR receivers want a 38 kHz carrier a WS2812 cannot make. ToF distance parts are centimetre-class.

**Camera or mouse sensor in the probe, LEDs as the pattern.** No need to store an image of the board: make the board its own fiducial. Give the sensor a pinhole or wide lens so it sees ~8–10 mm (a 3×3 or 4×3 block of holes); have the board show a binary brightness pattern (100 % / ~40 % of each hole's colour) in which every window is unique, a 2D de Bruijn-style code. 3×3 gives 512 windows for the 464 interior positions of a 60×10 grid but the probe rotates, so use 4×3, three levels, or an orientation cue. The frame also gives sub-hole position (the lattice's offset from the needle's fixed pixel, ~0.1 mm on a 36-pixel sensor). The pattern must be on while the frame is grabbed, so show it only while the probe is active. Sensors: PAW3204/3205 ($0.20–1, 18–19 px) have no documented pixel dump, avoid; ADNS-5050/3050 dump one pixel per frame, ~8 fps, prototype only; PMW3901 (~$3.80, 35×35, documented raw mode) is good but needs its own lens; PMW3360 ($6–10, 36×36, ~30 fps burst) is the best mouse part; GC0308/OV7670 ($1.50–3) or HM01B0 ($3–5, 320×320, low power) are real cameras and need parallel capture, so an RP2350-class probe. A printed Anoto-style dot code read through a stock mouse lens would need 0.3 mm dots, beyond an 18-pixel sensor. Matching against a stored image of the printed labels is possible with 320 pixels but is a research project next to a designed code and a lookup.

### 3.4 Magnetic

**DC magnet plus sparse 3-axis magnetometers (the other one chosen, §6).** A 6×3 mm N52 disc gives about 12 mT on its axis 10 mm from its face, 1.9 mT at 20 mm, 0.5 mT at 30 mm (from the closed-form on-axis field of a cylinder with Br = 1.45 T, and consistent with the dipole estimate 2m/r³ used in §6.4). Earth is 0.05 mT. The SNR near the tip is large, so the array can be sparse, ~20 mm pitch, about 12 sensors under a half-size breadboard at ~$0.30–0.50 each. A six-parameter dipole fit gives position, height and the magnet's axis, so the tip can be found from a magnet mounted up the barrel. The probe needs no battery to be located. Pain points: ambient drift (baseline subtraction), steel component leads that magnetise slightly, the magnet collecting clippings and tugging steel-legged parts, and addressing many identical I²C parts (§6.2). Originally the plan for Jerorack (github.com/Architeuthis-Flux/Jerorack), which has the KiCad design but no firmware yet.

**AC magnetic dipole plus PCB pickup coils (Wacom EMR with an active pen).** Probe: ~100 turns on a 2×8 mm ferrite, 10 mA at ~15 kHz, moment ~3×10⁻⁵ A·m². Board: 10 mm spiral coils, 4 layers × 10 turns, ~1 mV induced at 10 mm, muxed into one amplifier (gain ~500) and an ADC, I/Q magnitude in software. Position from neighbouring-coil amplitude ratios, sub-mm, real hover, tilt. Lock-in detection makes it blind to Earth's field, desk magnets and steel; at 15 kHz the phosphor-bronze clips are transparent (skin depth ~1.4 mm against 0.25 mm of metal) where at 500 kHz they would shield; several probes can use different frequencies. A passive LC tip would be battery-free and give pressure from a variable capacitor. Costs a coil driver in the probe, an analog front end on the board, and copper area on a PCB already full of LEDs. It is the same geometry and the same fit as the DC version with a lock-in in front, which is why DC is the one to prototype first.

### 3.5 Femtonyl: capacitive sensing from nothing

Femtonyl (github.com/Architeuthis-Flux/Femtonyl) times a pad's discharge with PIO to measure femtofarads. Used passively it tracks the *hand*, not the tip: the biggest conductor in range wins, so expect hand position to a couple of cm and a usable height. That is still worth having for wake-up and gestures. **Driven** is different: if the probe toggles its tip at a few kHz, each pad's discharge time shifts with the probe's instantaneous polarity, and a software lock-in per pad at the probe's frequency measures coupling to the probe specifically. Hand, LEDs and drift are not coherent at that frequency and drop out. Plausibly ±3–8 mm at a couple of cm of hover, height from total amplitude, two probes on two frequencies.

It may need no new copper: the crossbar can route a timing GPIO to whichever rows are free, chosen from the netlist. Parasitics fatten the baseline (5 pF becomes tens) and dilute ΔC/C; lock-in averaging buys most of it back. When the tip *touches* a free row the coupling jumps from femtofarads to galvanic, so a row scan finds the exact row with no second system. Side effects: plugging a part in slams those rows' baseline, so insertions are detectable for free; an undriven finger on a free row is a touch UI; and anything conductive touching the tip re-radiates the beacon, so take the loudest cluster rather than the sum.

The user's objection stands: breadboard clips may have parts on them, which interferes. Hence dedicated copper in the 2–3 unused strips under the probe pads, which is §5.

**The layering this points to.** No single method does everything cheaply, and the Pencil does not try to either (digitizer for position, a separate signal for "am I touching"). For V6: a *contact channel* is ground truth for the row (galvanic hit on a pad or a crossbar row scan); the *ratio ladder* or *magnetics* gives continuous position and hover; *LED codes* are nearly free if there is an LED per hole and only need to resolve a–e. The probe emits one square wave for all the capacitive layers at once.

## 5. The capacitive ratio ladder

A digital caliper is this exact effect as a product: a driven electrode sliding over patterned receive electrodes, resolving femtofarads to 0.01 mm in a bare fist on a coin cell. One millimetre at three times the gap is 100 times easier.

**Principle.** The probe drives its tip with a 3.3 V square wave at f0 ≈ 50 kHz. It floats; the return is hand → body → earth → board. The tip couples into the pad strip at x, the injected current divides toward the two ends of the ladder, and the board takes the lock-in (Goertzel) amplitude at each end:

```
x_norm = A_R / (A_L + A_R)
```

Coupling capacitance, hover height, grip, skin and battery voltage all scale both amplitudes equally. A hand without a carrier produces nothing coherent at f0. The sensing pads act as combs that collect the tip's field into the chain.

**Error budget for 1 mm over ~150 mm (0.7 % of ratio).**

- *SNR is not the limit.* ~0.05–0.1 pF tip-to-strip at 10 mm, 100 kHz, 3.3 V is 100–200 nA; into 1 MΩ that is 100–200 mV before lock-in gain, and a 5–10 ms window puts noise far under 0.1 %.
- *The ladder is an RC line.* Chain resistance with 2–5 pF per pad has a corner above which the division turns complex and position-dependent in phase. Keep total R in the low tens of kΩ for a 100 kHz carrier, or lower f0, or measure I/Q at both ends and calibrate (phase is then a second observable).
- *The injection is not a point.* This is the real systematic: barrel and hand couple in too. Drive only a small tip electrode and tie a barrel shield to probe ground; the hand rides probe ground, which barely moves (0.1 pF of tip pulling against ~100 pF of hand).
- *End effects, pad pitch, tilt* are static, so one calibration table, collected by an LED-guided "touch the lit hole" game.

**Using the 2–3 spare strips per flank.** (1) the ladder; (2) a grounded guard strip between it and the breadboard clips, which is what keeps the user's circuit out of the measurement and deserves a whole strip; (3) optionally a plain strip split into 3–4 zones on their own channels as a coarse check and hand detector. Strips on both flanks give the other axis from the left/right total-amplitude ratio, but that is a 1/distance-like ratio over a 15 mm span: expect ±1–2 mm, enough to call the column most of the time with contact or LEDs as tiebreaker. Height comes coarsely from total amplitude; contact shows as the amplitude jumping from femtofarad to galvanic coupling.

**Proof of concept on V5** (`ratio-ladder-poc-spec.md`): a floating Pico 2 as transmitter with a copper-tape tip and a grounded barrel shield, a second Pico 2 as receiver bodged to the ladder ends, N = 512-sample Goertzel bursts with a guard bin for SNR, a slot pair every 4–10 ms. Three receive schemes: **C** uses only the accessible (non-ground) ladder end, alternating it between floating-into-ADC and grounded-through-a-10 kΩ shunt, which needs no board surgery and validates the chain first; **A** is the symmetric one (ground one end, read the other, swap) and needs the hard-grounded end cut and bodged to a second ADC, approved in advance, with the contact-mode ground-truth sweep recorded *before* the cut because the cut breaks the stock divider; **B** grounds both ends through shunts and measures current, as the fallback if ADC loading distorts A. The experiments that decide it are ratio invariance under grip and battery changes, hand-only rejection, and accuracy with LEDs and a user circuit running. For V6, replace the cut with an analog switch so contact mode and ratio mode coexist.

**Later thinking on the receiver** (design notes §5): the CH32H417's TKEY block works but needs the same ADC_IN pins as plain sampling and has no DMA. Preferred is RP2350 PIO threshold timing on plain GPIOs (charge a ladder end through a pull-up, release, count to the Schmitt crossing, two state machines, DMA out), which keeps all 16 CH32 ADC inputs free.

## 6. The magnetometer array, as built on Testolomew

### 6.1 Hardware

A 4×2 array of TI TMAG5273 (SOT-23-6, I²C, 3-axis linear Hall, the symbol and footprint are in the Jerorack KiCad project) wired to a wuxx nanoCH32H417:

| Signal | Pin | Note |
|---|---|---|
| SCL | PA8 | I2C3, alternate function 4. The silicon pairs PA8 only with PC9 |
| SDA | PC9 | |
| Sensor VCC 0–3 | PD0, PC10, PC11, PA14 | Sensor 1 was first on PC12 and moved to PC10. The original wiring note listed PC12 twice; PA14 was a guess and a sensor does answer on it |
| Sensor VCC 4–7 | PD6, PD2, PD3, PD4 | Sensor 4 was first on PD1 and moved to PD6 |
| Ground, set A / set B | PA15 / PD5 | driven low and left |
| INT | unwired | masked in the sensor (INT_CONFIG_1 MASK_INTB) |

Three things about this chip and board that are easy to lose an evening to:

- **I/O voltage.** On the CH32H417 only PA5–PA7 and PE2–PE6 are on the fixed 3.3 V rail. Nearly everything else, including every array pin except PA8, is in the VIO18 domain, a rail the chip generates itself and which powers up at **1.2 V**. The TMAG5273 needs 1.7 V. `PWR->CTLR[12:10]` selects it (0 = 1.2 V, 1 = 1.8 V, 2 = 2.5 V, 3 = 3.3 V) and it has to be set before any pin is configured. The CH32H4 Arduino core raises it to 3.3 V during boot, which is the only reason GPIO-powered 3.3 V sensors work here; WCH's SDK examples and ch32fun leave it to you. On the nano board the VIO_1.8 net connects to 3.3 V only through R12, which is not fitted, so the internal regulator is what supplies it.
- **No internal pull-ups on I²C.** Open-drain mode on this part offers none. SDA and SCL need real resistors to 3.3 V; without them the bus reads as permanently busy.
- **Sensor supply from a GPIO is TI's own recommendation** (datasheet 7.2.2, 5 mA per sensor), so this is a sanctioned circuit and not a hack.

**First bench results (2026-09-17).** The fitted parts are TMAG5273B1 (factory address 0x22, ±40/80 mT). With the bus healthy, six of the eight address and read cleanly at 100 Hz: no read failures, about ±0.03 mT of noise per axis at 16× averaging, die temperatures 31–34 °C. The sensors on PC12 (sensor 1) and PD1 (sensor 4) acknowledge nothing even when powered alone, so those two are a wiring or part problem, not firmware. The array was unreliable at first because the SDA pull-up made intermittent contact: the `b` bus check showed SDA floating (it followed the chip's weak internal pull-up and pull-down, and never rose after release) in exactly the runs where nothing addressed, and pulled up with a ~2 µs rise in the runs where everything worked.

**The bench array's geometry.** It is hand wired on two SMD proto boards: four sensors along the top about 15 mm apart and four along the bottom 45 mm below them, parts on the top side, the lower board turned 180° relative to the upper one (so the two rows' packages probably face opposite ways). The firmware first assumed a 20 × 20 mm grid, and with that table no mounting hypothesis fitted consistently (best misfits wandered 5–40 % and the winner changed with every magnet position), which is what a wrong *position* table looks like, as opposed to a wrong orientation. In simulation this layout gives sub-millimetre fixes over a row and about 0.5 mm between the rows up to 15 mm height with a 6×3 mm N52; a 4×2 mm magnet is good to about 10 mm; a 2.5×1.5 mm magnet only fixes within ~10 mm of a row, because the other row never sees it.

**Orientation and row order.** Every package is on the top side with pin 1 at the bottom left, which is rotation 270 in the table's convention (the pin 1-2-3 side faces −y, pin 1→3 runs toward +x). The first `o` run fitted row 4–7 at 270 but wanted row 0–3 turned half way round (90), at 23 % misfit. For a single straight row, "turned half way round" and "wired in the opposite order" are the same thing, and the packages are known to match, so row 0–3 is taken to run right to left (sensor 0 at the right-hand end). `o` was then extended to test each row's order as well: 256 combinations, about 15 s. With row order in play every answer appears four times (itself, its two mirror images, and the frame turned half way round), so the side the parts are on and one package's rotation are what pick the line. Not yet confirmed with a magnet on the bench.

**Correction to the paragraph above, from measurement.** The row-order inference was wrong and the rotation finding was right. Sweeping a magnet counter-clockwise, `i` reported 3,2,1,0 then 4,5,6,7, so both rows run left to right; and Bx flips the same way on both rows although the magnet crosses them in opposite directions, which only oppositely turned packages do. The far row really is at 90° and the near row at 270° (the near proto board is the far one turned half way round, so "pin 1 bottom left" is true on each board and opposite between them).

**Self-calibration (`tools/magcal`).** Hand-entered positions were the limit, so the array was calibrated from data: a 200 s recording of the `f` stream while a strong magnet (fitted moment ≈ 127,000 mT·mm³) was waved over the array at 3–6 cm. `magcal` alternates between fitting the magnet pose in every frame (with the firmware's own MagFit) and moving, turning and scaling each sensor to best explain its own readings, with sensor 4 at the origin, sensor 7 on the x axis, and one caliper measurement for scale (sensor 4 to 7 = 53.4 mm; fields fix the array's shape but not its size). Result: gaps of 16 / 22 / 16 mm along each row, not 15 / 15 / 15; rows 44.3 mm apart (independently, Kevin's estimate was 45); rotations 85–92° and 271–272°; gains within ±3 %. Mean dipole misfit on frames held out of the calibration fell from 10.7 % to 2.4 %. The table in `MagArrayConfig.h` now holds these values.

**First accuracy measurement on hardware (2026-09-17, ruler test).** A small magnet (fitted moment ≈ 4,500, about a 5×2.5 mm N52) on a ~30 mm spacer, slid along a ruler with a stop at every 10 mm mark, by hand:

| | result |
|---|---|
| 10 mm steps along x, over the near row (6 steps) | 10.8, 9.5, 9.7, 9.8, 9.6, 9.4 mm: mean 9.8, 58.6 mm for a true 60 |
| 10 mm steps along y, between the rows (4 steps) | 8.2, 9.7, 10.7, 10.0 mm |
| 10 mm steps along y, beyond the far row | 11.1, 11.5, 12.8 mm: scale stretches outside the array's footprint |
| height during the x sweep | 31.4 → 29.1 mm (spacer not level, or residual calibration error; not separated) |
| scatter of a hand-held "stationary" magnet | 0.9 mm RMS in xy, 0.5 mm in z, slow, so a speed-adaptive (1-euro) output filter only brought it to 0.55 mm: much of it is probably the hand |
| fitted moment across positions | 3,800–5,000 inside the footprint, rising to 6,300 outside: it should be constant, so this is the residual model error showing |
| fit time while tracking | 1–3 ms; residual 0.006–0.012 mT |

So: steps good to about ±0.5 mm and scale to ~2 % inside the footprint, at 30 mm height with a weak magnet, which is the noise-limited corner of the table in §6.5. Lower height or a stronger magnet is where sub-millimetre should come from. The obvious next improvement is to fix the magnet's moment once it is known (a probe has one magnet): that removes the height/strength trade-off visible in the last row.

**Range and noise, measured.** With no magnet near, 32× averaging, RMS over all eight sensors: ±80 mT range 17.7 / 17.2 / 9.9 µT on X / Y / Z; ±40 mT range 17.4 / 17.2 / 8.7 µT. So the narrow range does not make the sensor more sensitive: the noise is the Hall element's, not the converter's step (2.4 µT per count at ±80, 1.2 at ±40). The array now runs at ±40 mT anyway, since the probe's magnet is meant to be weak, and a sensor that reaches 95 % of full scale is left out of that frame's fit (it shows orange on the LCD and `SATURATED` in `m`). What would actually buy sensitivity to a weak magnet is a quieter kind of sensor: AMR/TMR magnetometers (MMC5603, MMC5983MA, QMC5883L class) are around 0.05–0.2 µT RMS, about a hundred times quieter than this Hall part, at the cost of a full scale of only ±0.8 to ±3 mT, so they saturate when a magnet comes within a centimetre or so. For a deliberately weak probe magnet that trade may be the right one for V6; a mixed array (Hall for near, AMR for far) is another option.

**More sensitivity from the datasheet.** The on-chip levers were already at their maximum (32× averaging; low-noise mode, which TI's own table shows makes no difference at 32×; and the range, which was measured to make none). What was left is what TI calls "the averaging that can be done in the microcontroller": at 32× a sensor finishes a result every 2.4 ms with X, Y and Z enabled (3.2 ms with the temperature channel as well: 825 µs for the first channel plus 800 µs for each further one), and the firmware was reading each sensor only ~65 times a second, so four of every five results were never read. Now the sensors are read as often as the loop allows and each published frame is the average of those reads. Three changes made that affordable: the chip's "1-byte read" mode (`I2C_RD = 01`: a bare read returns the enabled channels and the status with no register address written first, a quarter less bus traffic; ordinary register reads stop working in that mode, so `tmag5273Begin` switches back to standard reads before it configures, and presence is checked by address acknowledge), the temperature channel off (`MAG_READ_TEMPERATURE`), and LCD bands of 15 rows instead of 30 so the display holds the loop for 2.4 ms at a time. Measured, no magnet near: a full array read fell from ~2.5 to 1.6 ms, each frame averages 2.9 reads, and per-frame noise went from 17.4 / 17.2 / 8.7 µT to **11.1 / 11.1 / 5.8 µT** (X / Y / Z) while the frame rate rose from 65 to 76 Hz. X and Y remain twice as noisy as Z; TI's remedy for that (XZX-style pseudo-simultaneous sampling) exists only for two-axis modes. The next step in this direction would be to sample from the second core (the V3F) so that the display never delays a read; beyond that it is hardware (closer sensors, or AMR/TMR parts as above).

**Known magnet strength.** `magFitSolveKnownStrength` runs the free fit and then refines position and direction with the strength held (softly, as one extra equation weighted so that a 1 % strength error costs as much as a 5 % field misfit). In simulation on the bench geometry with the ruler-test magnet it about halves the height noise (1.66 → 0.87 mm RMS at 30 mm) and leaves x/y where it was (3.57 → 3.48 mm), because x/y noise does not come from the height/strength trade-off; being 5 % wrong about the strength costs little. On the console `k` learns the strength (median of 150 good free fixes) and holds it, `K` forgets it, and `MAGLOC_MAGNET_STRENGTH` makes it permanent.

**"No fit" in places, and what it turned out not to be (2026-09-17, recording `2026-09-17-all-orientations.txt`).** Reported as no fit between sensors 1–2 and 5–6. A recording with a stronger magnet (fitted moment ≈ 20,000) held still in those spots and then waved about at every tilt showed misfit tracking the magnet's *tilt*: 2–4 % upright, 22 % lying down, with the fitted strength falling from 22,600 to 11,000–16,800. Each explanation was then tested against the data rather than argued:

| hypothesis | test | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| sensor positions too loose for close range | misfit against field strength in the first recording | no growth up close |
| the magnet is long, not a point dipole | refit every frame with a two-pole magnet of length L, 0–40 mm | best at L = 0, worse with every step |
| the solver finds a wrong minimum for a flat dipole | 27,000 cold starts on the bench geometry with perfect data, tilts 0–90° | zero wrong answers |
| one sensor axis reads hot (X/Y and Z are different Hall structures) | rescale each axis array-wide, 0.6–1.6 | all three best at exactly 1.0 |
| the sensors' zero moved after a strong magnet was near (hysteresis in nearby steel, drift) | quiet frames before against after | unchanged to 0.01 mT |
| a fuller per-sensor response (free 3×3 matrix) | `magcal` extended to solve it | runs away: the regression comes out slightly small, the magnet fits retreat, repeat. Removed again |
| re-calibrate position/rotation/gain on this recording | `magcal`, held-out test | no improvement (20 % → 20 %), positions go wild, held-out worse |

What the frames do show: the difference between measured and modelled field is about the same *absolute* size, 0.03–0.1 mT per axis, whether the fields are 7 mT or 0.5 mT. So whenever at least one sensor is in a strong field the fit is excellent (1.5–4 %, and that sensor dominates), and whenever every sensor is in the weak far field the same 0.05–0.1 mT is a fifth of the signal and the height/strength trade-off takes over (it picks closer-and-weaker). The tilt correlation was incidental: the lying-down stretches were also the far, weak-signal ones. That error is three to ten times the sensor noise, is not zero drift, and is not something moving, turning or rescaling the sensors can explain, so it is a field that a single dipole does not describe: most likely soft iron near the array being magnetised by the magnet (the nano board's RJ45 jack and USB shells sit about 2 cm from the far row, where the worst single errors were, and the pin headers may be steel), or steel or a second magnet in whatever held the magnet. Not settled. The clean test is a bare magnet held in fingers or plastic, with the array moved away from the dev board.

Practical consequences for now: the misfit limit is 40 % so that these cases give a rough fix instead of none, and every fix carries its misfit and, better, its own error bar (next paragraph).

**The error bar.** Every fix now carries a 1-σ position error per axis, in millimetres, computed by the fit itself: at the answer it takes the Jacobian J of the readings with respect to the magnet's position (with the moment's freedom already projected out in the free fit, and with the strength equation included in the known-strength fit, which is what shrinks σz there) and the residual variance s² (never allowed below 0.005 mT per reading), and reports the square roots of the diagonal of s²(JᵀJ)⁻¹. It was checked two ways. In simulation, where the true error is known, predicted and actual agree to within a few percent from 10 to 40 mm height for both fits, and the truth falls inside 1σ 66–71 % of the time and inside 2σ 92–97 % (a unit test holds it to that). On the ruler-test recording, where the magnet sat still at each mark, the measured scatter of single-frame fits was 1.7–2.9 mm in x, 1.9–4.4 in y and 0.9–2.1 in z, against predictions of 1.9–3.4, 2.0–3.1 and 1.6–2.0. It treats the residual as random, so an error that repeats in every frame (a sensor in the wrong place, steel nearby) is only partly counted: read it as "at least this uncertain". The console's `l` prints x ± σ for each axis, the fix CSV has three σ columns, the LCD shows ± mm on the top line and draws a 2-σ ellipse on the board under the magnet and a 2-σ bar on its height, and a fix whose combined bar exceeds 15 mm (`MAGLOC_MAX_ERROR_MM`) is not offered at all.

**Weak magnets: a fix on faint readings (2026-09-17).** (The presence threshold was 0.06 mT when this was written; it is 0.04 since the lattice search.) The locator used to wait for three sensors reading above 0.04 mT before it tried a fit, and a weak magnet close to one row gives two such sensors and a few neighbours at 0.02 mT: "only 2 sensors see it". The count was only ever a gate on spending the time, though. The fit has always been handed every working sensor, however little it reads, and a reading of almost nothing is information too (it says the magnet is not near). So the gate now counts sensors that notice the magnet at all, above `MAGLOC_FAINT_MT` = 0.015 mT, three times the smoothed noise; three are needed, and the error bar decides whether the answer is worth having. `l` and the row readout print both counts ("seen by 2+3 faint"). Simulated on the bench geometry with the smoothed noise, fixes with only two sensors above 0.04 mT land within half a row 86–99 % of the time (x error 0.3–1.0 mm rms), and the error bar stays on the cautious side. The catch is the unexplained 0.03–0.1 mT discrepancy below: a faint sensor's whole reading is that size. With 0.02 mT of it added to the simulation, the same fixes are 2–4 mm out and the bar under-reports by up to half. Faint fixes are worth having on a clean array and not yet on this one. One side effect: near the edge of the array more fits now run and then fail the 15 mm error-bar limit, and each failure means another 49 ms cold start 200 ms later, so the LCD frame rate dips there; that is the gate working.

**Row counting: the sub-2.54 mm test (`src/rowcount`).** The question the magnetic probe has to answer is which breadboard row it is over, so there is a mode for exactly that. The breadboard is the Jumperless one: rows 1–30 along the far half, 31–60 along the near half, row 31 facing row 1 across the centre channel, five holes per row running away from the channel, 7.62 mm between the two innermost holes. `RowGrid` (pure math, host-tested) turns a position into how far along the board it is, in rows (14.0 is level with the centres of rows 14 and 44), and how far across from the channel's centre line in mm; the half with rows 1–30 is always to the left of the direction the numbers count up in, so the sign of "across" picks the half and its size picks the hole (1 = next to the channel … 5). Up to one row past either end still counts as the end row; further is "off the end". It also turns the fix's error bar into rows and into the chance that the nearest row is the true one (the part of a bell curve of that width inside the row's ±0.5, times the chance the half is right: 95 % at σ = 0.25 rows dead centre, a coin toss on a boundary however good the fix). `RowCounter` keeps two positions apart on purpose: each frame's raw fix, which is the one the error bar describes, gives the place, σ and confidence; the counted row follows the display-smoothed fix and only changes 0.1 row past a boundary (1 mm past the channel line), so it does not chatter. `r` shows the counted row large on the LCD (green above 95 % sure, yellow above 68 %, orange below), draws both halves of the breadboard with the counted row white, and prints a line twice a second. `h` is the measurement: two seconds of fixes while the probe is held still give the mean place, the scatter of single fixes in rows and mm next to the fit's own σ, the scatter of 8-fix averages (what more smoothing would buy; little when the signal is weak, because the fields are already smoothed then), the share of single fixes that called the row, and the move since the previous `h`. Probe in a hole, `h`, next hole, `h`: the move should read +1.00 rows every time, all the way along. Where the breadboard is comes from anchors: fixes taken with the probe in known holes, all fitted together by weighted least squares (each anchor weighted by its own error bar). How much is fitted depends on what the anchors can pin down. One anchor slides the breadboard into place. Two or more at least 10 mm apart also give its angle and one scale, which takes out how the breadboard lies on the array and the magnet fit's scale error. Four or more spread both along and across give a scale each way and a skew as well. `R<row>` adds one anchor by hand (hole next to the channel, any row 1–60), and every one given is kept. `c` is the guided version: it asks for twelve taps, rows 1, 15, 30, 60, 45 and 31, each at the hole next to the channel and at the outermost hole, shows the wanted hole on the LCD, and detects each tap by itself: the fix staying put for a second (within three of its own error bars, the first half of the second discarded as the smoothing settling) at the height the breadboard holds the magnet at, which the first tap teaches it, and at least 5 mm from the previous tap. Because the breadboard is fixed to the array, that resting height is a constant of the setup (`ROWCOUNT_TOUCH_Z_MM`; the readout says "down" or "up" by it). After the fit it prints what every anchor still misses its hole by, along in rows and across in mm, and the rms. That residual is the accuracy of the whole scheme: it is the part of the magnet fit's error that no straight-line map removes, and it has to stay well under 1.27 mm for rows to be told apart. It also prints the grid as a `ROWCOUNT_GRID_AT_BOOT` line to paste into `RowCounter.h`, since nothing is stored across a reset. Host simulation of the whole chain, with the "real" sensors deliberately 2 % and 1.5 % off the firmware's table and 0.3 mm out each: all twelve taps detected (a dither in mid-air was not), residual 0.71 mm rms, and afterwards 59 of 60 rows counted right at the middle hole, the wrong one being row 2 read as 2.50; the left-over error is a bow of up to half a row over the first rows of the far half, which is the non-linearity a straight-line map cannot take out.

**Row mode, first run on hardware (2026-09-17).** A breadboard laid over the bench array, the magnet slid along it by hand at a steady 16 mm above the sensors (fitted height 15.5–17 mm throughout, which is itself a good sign), default grid then `R1` (console log kept as `tools/recordings/2026-09-17-row-mode-first-run.txt`). Row 1 anchored at x = −10.1 mm and the far end of the rows came out at x ≈ 64–65, so the breadboard overhangs the 0–54 mm sensor array by about 10 mm at each end. Over the array, with all eight sensors seeing the magnet plainly, the fix's own error bar is 0.04–0.10 rows (0.1–0.25 mm) and successive readings with the magnet still agree to about 0.1 row (14.47 … 14.57); misfit 3–5 %. Over the overhanging ends it is 0.2–0.35 rows with readings wandering ±0.2 rows, misfit 6–9 %, four to six sensors seeing it plainly and the rest faintly. Lifting the magnet to 30–40 mm takes the bar past a whole row. So at this height the resolution is well under a row everywhere on the breadboard and about ten times under it in the middle. What this run did not measure is accuracy: the magnet was not stepped hole to hole, so there is no true position to compare with. The far end read 30.3–30.6 rows where the breadboard stops at 30, which is what a fit reading 1–2 % long beyond the last sensors would do (the ruler test stretched out there too), but which row the magnet was really on was not recorded. Simulation agrees about the ends: a perfect dipole with the measured noise gives 0.10–0.23 rows of scatter at x = 65 against 0.03–0.07 in the middle, with no bias (mean error under 0.05 mm at the smoothed noise level), and because the field smoothing turns that noise into a slow wander, even a 2 s average out there is only good to about 0.1–0.2 rows. The lesson for the V6 layout is that the sensors should reach the ends of the rows, not stop 10 mm short. The faint-reading gate did what it was meant to: fixes with one to three sensors seeing the magnet plainly came through with bars of 1–2.5 rows, marked as such, instead of "no fix". This run is also what showed the first version's numbering to be wrong for a breadboard (it counted one line of rows along x, so the near half repeated the far half's numbers). A second run with the two-half numbering (weaker magnet, ±0.5–0.9 rows at the overhanging ends) used `R30` and then `R1` expecting the pair to set the scale; the firmware of the time only moved the origin on each `R`, which left row 30 reading 33–34. That is what made every anchor count in one fit.

**Brief "no fix" dropouts while tracking (2026-09-17).** The second row-mode log shows the fix dropping out for a moment once or twice a second at spots where it was otherwise fine (4–14 % misfit, bars of 0.2–0.9 rows), which would have stopped the tap detector ever seeing a full still second. What trips it on the hardware has not been captured yet (the `d` stream records invalid fits with their misfit and error bar, and would show it). Two things were found in the code and fixed. First, one bad set of readings cost far more than one frame: the weak-signal smoothing kept it in the fields for about ten frames, none of which fit, and the first failure dropped the locator into the cold-start pacing, another 200 ms. In simulation one glitched frame in forty left the locator without a fix 35 % of the time. Now every sensor axis goes through a median of its last three frames before the smoothing (the I²C reads carry no checksum, so a corrupted read is a real possibility on this wiring; cost: one frame of delay), and while tracking, up to two frames in a row may fail to fit before the magnet is called lost, the next frame starting from the last good position. With both, the same simulation loses no frames to glitches at all. Second, the presence threshold has a little stickiness, so a weak magnet right at 0.06 mT does not flicker in and out. The tap detector itself also tolerates gaps in the fixes under 0.3 s and up to ten stray fixes in a row.

**Two magnets on the probe (2026-09-17, simulation only so far).** The idea: a small magnet right at the tip and a stronger one further up the shaft. It is fitted as one rigid thing (`src/magfit/MagProbeFit`): both magnets on the probe's axis, a known distance apart, magnetised along it. The search is over five numbers, the tip magnet's position and the axis direction, where the single magnet's is over three; the field is linear in the two signed strengths, so variable projection carries over with a 2×2 solve in place of the 3×3. The axis is carried as (ax, ay) with az = +√(1 − ax² − ay²), smooth at vertical; the cold start fits a single dipole first (two magnets in line look like one magnet between them) and tries the probe along it and upright. A general two-free-dipole fit (twelve unknowns) was not built: the array cannot support it and it is not what a probe is. What simulation on the bench array says, tip magnet 14 mm above the sensors, strong magnet 4500 mT·mm³, smoothed noise, tips over the whole breadboard, tip x error rms:

| | strong magnet 15 mm up | strong magnet 25 mm up |
|---|---|---|
| strong magnet alone, tip = magnet − spacing × its axis | 0.30 mm | 1.0 mm (78–88 % right row) |
| …with its strength known | 0.25 mm | 0.9 mm |
| tip magnet alone (300 / 600 / 1000) | 2.4 / 1.3 / 0.75 mm | the same |
| both, strengths fitted freely | 0.3–1.5 mm | 0.7–1.6 mm |
| both, strengths known | 0.2 mm | 0.33–0.5 mm (97–100 % right row) |

So the second magnet is worth a factor of two to three in tip error when the strong magnet has to sit 25 mm up the shaft, nothing much when it can sit at 15 mm, and nothing at all unless both strengths are known: with them free, the split between the two magnets is one more thing for the noise to push around. The reason the single strong magnet does badly is that the tip is (distance up the shaft) × (error in the probe's angle) away from the magnet, and the angle is the worst-determined thing in the fit. The same shows in the two-magnet fit's own numbers: the tip is good to half a millimetre while the tilt wanders by ±15°. Strengths told wrong by 30 % (tip) and 5 % (back) cost 0.33 → 0.67 mm, so learned values are good enough. Learning them from the two-magnet fit itself, free, over a ten-second wave, comes out within about 6 % on the tip magnet and 1–5 % low on the back one (measuring each magnet alone with `k` before it goes on the probe is better); fitting the spacing too is badly biased (25 → 28–32 mm), so the spacing is asked for, by caliper. Magnets facing opposite ways fit, but cold starts then land in wrong minima (1.6 mm rms): mount both the same way round. The shaft has to be non-magnetic (wood, plastic, brass, aluminium): a steel one is soft iron a few millimetres from both magnets and bends the very field the fit models. The spacing need not be measured finely: told 0.5 mm wrong it costs nothing (0.34 → 0.35 mm), 1.5 mm wrong 0.45 mm, 3.5 mm wrong 0.75 mm, so the nearest millimetre, which is what `t` takes, is enough. The held strengths are signed (+ = north toward the back of the probe), and only the two-magnet learn finds the signs; a magnet measured alone with `k` gives a better magnitude, to be put in with the learned sign. Through the real locator and row counter on the host, a probe tilted 15° with a 300 to 1000 tip magnet counted 60 of 60 rows right (tip x error 0.25–0.39 mm rms) after learning its own strengths. Cost: on the host a tracking fit is 3.6 times the single magnet's and a cold start 3.7 times; on the chip they measured 1–2 ms and 105 ms. Tracking is capped at four iterations a frame, cold starts are paced at 600 ms, and a fit slower than 6 ms would run on every other frame. `l` prints the fit time and both fitted strengths. Console: `t<mm>` switches it on, `T` learns again.

**Two magnets on the probe, first try on hardware (2026-09-17).** The probe as built had its magnets stuck to the side of the shaft, so their poles face out from it, not along it. The first version of the fit assumed magnets magnetised along the shaft (a magnet on the end of a rod), and it showed: fitted as one magnet the probe read a tilt of 74–90° with the strength jumping between 10,000 and 25,000 from one pose to the next, and the two-magnet fit put its "tip" 33–35 mm to the side of where the probe touched the board (row 1 at x −43 where the single-magnet calibration had it at −10), which is exactly the spacing that was typed in: the fit had laid the probe flat along the sideways-pointing field. The model now lets each magnet be magnetised along the shaft or across it; an across magnet brings the probe's roll about its own shaft into the pose (six searched numbers instead of five), the roll being referred to the shaft's x-most perpendicular so that vertical is not a special case. The learning step fits all four mountings side by side on every frame, with strengths free, and takes the one with the lowest middle misfit, printing all four so the choice can be seen. Host tests: every mounting is found exactly from a cold start at every pose tried, and the wrong one fits at least three times worse; an across probe locates its tip about as well as an along one (0.58 against 0.52 mm). Through the real locator on the host, the probe as the log suggests it is (both magnets across, about 4,000 and 14,000, 35 mm apart) is learned as such at 1.7 % misfit against 10–23 % for the other mountings, and counts 60 of 60 rows with 0.10 mm of tip error; a weak across tip magnet (300) or a mixed mounting is identified too but loses lock now and then (3 of 60 rows wrong). On the chip the two-magnet tracking fit measured 1.0–2.0 ms and its cold start 105 ms, so it runs on every frame; learning runs four fits a frame and paces the failing mountings' cold starts. The sign of a learned strength is arbitrary for an across magnet (turning it round is the same as rolling the probe half a turn), so a negative number there is not a mistake.

**Second try, and where the strong magnet belongs (2026-09-17).** With the across mounting in the model, the learn picked "tip along, back across" at 7.7 % misfit, with the back magnet at 15,800 and the tip magnet at −55 ± 100: the tip magnet was not being seen at all. Every fix then failed the 15 mm error-bar limit (bars of 17–85 mm), correctly: with only the back magnet seen, the tip is the back magnet's position carried 35 mm down a shaft whose direction is the worst-known thing in the fit. Simulation with the bench's noise floor (0.004 mT smoothed plus the 0.03 mT discrepancy) and that back magnet says how strong a tip magnet has to be: 1,000 gives 3 mm of tip error, 4,000 gives 0.9 mm (84 % right row), 8,000 gives 0.5 mm, 15,800 gives 0.28 mm - and with the strong magnet AT THE TIP the back magnet hardly matters (1,000 to 15,800 all give 0.27–0.29 mm). So on this array the premise is the other way round: the strong magnet goes at the tip, where it is close to the sensors and pins the point that matters, and the back magnet only has to be strong enough to give the shaft's direction. The learn now says so when the tip magnet comes out at under 5 % of the back one. The across cold start was also cut from 24 probe seeds to 9 (the single fit's pole gives the roll), after a 300 ms cold start showed in the log.

**Third try, and the decision (2026-09-17, 17:50).** With the general model (any angle to the shaft, the back magnet turned any way round it, spacing learned) the fit reproduced, pose by pose, exactly what two free dipoles find: 4–6 % misfit per held pose, magnets about 26 mm apart, both across the shaft, 70–115° apart round it, the tip one about 3,400 and the back one 5,000–7,000. But learning from single frames of a wave came out with the spacing anywhere from 35 to 58 mm and the angles off by 40°, and a single rigid shape fitted jointly to three held poses left 6–8 % against the 4–6 % of fitting each pose on its own: no one shape explains all the poses to the noise. The reason is the size of things. The two magnets make 0.1–0.3 mT at the sensors, and the array carries about 0.05 mT of unexplained, repeatable error (see "no fit" above); at that ratio there is no telling a slightly different shape from a slightly wrong sensor, and with the fit at its floor the second magnet buys nothing. Kevin called it: the two-magnet fit is dropped (the code is in `attic/two-magnet/`) and the probe's orientation comes from the ONE magnet's pole. That needs a magnet magnetised along the shaft - on the end of the rod, or threaded on it - not a disc on its side; its pole direction is then the shaft, the point is `MAGLOC_TIP_OFFSET_MM` down it, and "down" is the lower end (a probe is not held upside down), so there is no sign to get wrong. The tip error is then (offset) × (the axis error), which is why the offset should be as short as the probe allows. What would make a second magnet worth revisiting is a lower array floor: sensor positions calibrated with a magnet 15–25 mm away rather than 30–60, and re-zeroing before use (the baseline had drifted by up to 0.1 mT in ten minutes on the bench).

**Bus glitches.** The hand wiring is marginal at 400 kHz. Now and then one transaction fails, typically while a freshly powered sensor is being configured, and leaves the chip's I²C block wedged so that nothing answers afterwards; at boot that showed up as "0 of 8" with sensor 0 reporting *moved to its new address, but configuring it failed* and the rest *nothing at any factory address*, and in use as every sensor dropping out at once. It happened on five flashes in a row and then on none of the next eight, with no relevant change in between. The firmware now gives each sensor three tries in the addressing walk with the I²C block re-initialised between them, re-initialises it at once when every sensor fails in the same frame (they are still powered and still addressed, so nothing else is needed), and `m` reports how often that has happened and, for a sensor that would not address, which step failed, on which register, with which Wire error code.

**Fit cost on the chip.** A cold-start fit (seven seeds) measures about 49 ms on the V5F, not the under-10 ms first estimated; tracking fits are a few iterations. Cold starts are rate-limited to 5 Hz so a magnet that cannot be fitted does not stall the loop. (Superseded below: the cold start now begins with a lattice search.)

**The overnight work of 2026-09-17/18: a tracker, a far-field estimate, the cursor, LEDs and an on-screen UI.** Kevin asked for the prior art on getting a better signal out of the magnetometers and for five things: a rough position when the probe is far away, a filter for motion that cannot be real, inference from past movement through gaps, a choice between the cursor sitting under the tip and where the tip points, and the pointing ray stopping at the breadboard's surface (7.1 mm above the base PCB on V6) instead of running on through it; plus the cursor on the breadboard's own LEDs and a display with a menu and camera controls. The study is `magnetometer-fusion-prior-art.md`; what was built from it is below. None of it has met a real magnet yet: the probe was away from the array all night, so everything past the fit was verified on the host (the whole firmware runs there, `pio test -e native` plus a simulation of the chain) and by driving the flashed board over the console.

**Far away: the gradient-tensor formula, tried and dropped.** The closed-form dipole locator of Nara, Suzuki and Ando (position from the field and its gradient tensor at one point, `G r = -3 B`, with the out-of-plane gradients from Maxwell) was implemented and its sign convention verified (a magnet 150 mm up is found within 4 mm from perfect readings). On this array it is no use: the gradient is a finite difference across 54 × 44 mm, and at every distance where there is any signal that is not a derivative - 25-50 % of the distance in error at 50-70 mm with *no* noise, 10 % only beyond 90 mm where a probe magnet reads 0.006 mT (`attic/far-field-gradient/`). It would work on a small, dense gradiometer; it will not work on a breadboard-sized array. What replaced it is a lattice search inside the fit (`magFitCoarse`): the dipole moment is solved at each point of a 25 mm lattice over and around the array (about 180 points), then 3 × 3 × 3 lattices of half the step round the best down to 3 mm - about 250 trial points, each half the cost of a fit iteration's evaluation because only the cost is wanted. It never iterates, cannot get stuck, and lands within a few millimetres of the answer wherever the magnet is: on the host, 2-4 mm rms up to 40 mm height and 8-14 mm at 40-60 mm with the bench noise, where the field is at the noise floor anyway. It is also now the first thing every cold start does: one refinement from the lattice's best point is usually the whole cold start (the seven old seeds are only tried when that comes out low, under 12 mm, where the false minimum pinned under a sensor lives and can fit to 4.6 %), which cut the cold start's iterations from 150-200 to 38 on average (host count; the chip time is to be read off `l` with a magnet). The presence threshold dropped from 0.06 to 0.04 mT to go with it, and a fit whose error bar is wider than 5 mm is passed on as a ROUGH fix - "somewhere about here" - rather than a position: no row is counted from it and the LEDs show a wide dim glow.

**The tracker (`src/magfit/MagTracker`).** A Kalman filter with a constant-velocity model, one per axis, on the stream of fixes; each fix weighs by its own error bar, floored at 0.3 mm for the array's systematic error and widened by (2 - α)/α for the field smoothing that made it (fixes made of fields smoothed over 1/α frames repeat most of each other's noise; a filter fed them as independent would grow overconfident), while the track's reported bar is never claimed wider than the last fix's, since fixes are still coming in. Process noise is a hand's acceleration, 3 m/s² (the menu's "hand accel"). A fix further than 4σ from where the track expects it, or implying more than 3 m/s since the last, is dropped - but after three dropped fixes in a row that agree with each other the track restarts from them, because a track can be wrong too. With no fix the track coasts for 400 ms with its velocity dying away (τ 0.15 s) and its bar growing, then gives up; a rough fix is a fix with a wide bar and keeps a far probe on the map. The shaft is smoothed separately and more heavily (τ 0.12 s), a swing over 45° in one frame waiting three frames to be believed, because the direction is the noisiest thing the fit gives and the pointer multiplies its error by the drop to the board. The cursor is then the tip carried down to the surface plane - straight down (`u`: under) or along the shaft (pointed) - never past the plane, never more than 40 mm from the tip (a probe held high and nearly level cannot throw it across the board), a tip below the plane being its own cursor; and it goes through a 1-Euro filter (Casiez, Roussel and Vogel 2012: a low-pass whose cutoff opens with speed; 1 Hz at rest, β 0.1 per mm/s), which is what makes it sit still in a hole and follow a moving hand with 1.7 mm of lag at 300 mm/s. Host tests: at rest the cursor scatters 0.14 mm against 0.5 mm in the fixes; a 30 mm teleport is dropped and a real jump followed on the third agreeing fix; a 200 ms gap is coasted through and the returning fix believed; and the whole chain on a simulated hand loop with the bench noise, a corrupted reading every 40 frames and a 150 ms dropout every 3 s gives fixes 3.8 mm rms, the track 1.1 mm and the cursor 0.8 mm, 26 glitched fixes dropped, none lost. The counted row follows the track's cursor and rides through gaps. Two things the tracker cannot do: it cannot know where the probe went during a gap longer than its coast, and the coasting bar grows fast (about 12 mm in 150 ms at weak signal) because the widened covariance makes the velocity uncertain - honest, and tunable. A ten-minute soak of a random hand on the host (random goals, 3 m/s² accelerations, a 0.3 mT glitch on one axis every 40 frames, a 140 ms dropout every 10 s) found no NaNs and no stuck states, but it did find that the weak-signal field smoothing lags a moving probe: fixes 8 mm behind a hand at 80 mm/s with a confident 0.7 mm bar, because the fields they were made of were 100 ms old. So the smoothing now backs off with the track's own speed (none above 50 mm/s, `MAGLOC_ALPHA_PER_MM_S`; at rest, where noise matters and lag does not, the weak-signal smoothing is back), which took the soak's tracking error from 2.9 to 0.9 mm rms.

**What the overnight review found.** Eight reviewers (one per area) and two skeptics per finding went over the new code on the host. The defects that stood and were fixed: a menu-run command that changed a setting (`g`, `u`, `r`...) was undone at once by the menu's own copy of that setting; the log screen could keep showing the previous screen's frame; `T` had no `<number>` in its help and would have blocked the menu for eight seconds; a rough fix could be offered with the *previous* frame's position under this frame's error bar; a sensor that missed frames during the baseline got a scaled-down baseline (a phantom field from then on); a clean baseline could be thrown away by a retake still pending from a polluted one; a sensor back from a bus dropout ran on stale median/smoothing history; the tracker's restart-on-agreeing-outliers could never fire on a moving hand (the three dropped fixes had to be within 6 mm of each other; they now have to be collinear and no faster than a hand, and the restarted track takes its velocity from them); a track born from rough fixes called itself coasting for its first 400 ms; the LED gamma rounded every dim glow to zero (a blend with the straight level and a floor keep it visible); the pointed-mode tail could outshine and overwrite a dim cursor; the V5 column order and rail sides were the wrong way round against JumperlOS's own tables (evidence noted in `ProbeLeds.h`; the V5 port does not depend on them); the V5 overlay's name has to be one JumperlOS treats as session-only or every paint marks the board state dirty; a NaN in a streamed cursor line would have poisoned every LED for good; and a lone ESC, CRLF after a typed number, and `millis()` wrap all had edge cases in the key emulation. Things behind the camera are no longer drawn mirrored (culled, not clipped).

**The baseline, two ways.** A baseline taken with the probe lying on the board subtracts the magnet from every reading after it, and after a reflash that is easy to do by accident. Now each new baseline, less its mean across the sensors (the earth's field and the average offset), is fitted as a dipole: sensor offsets are random from sensor to sensor and do not fit one, a magnet does, so a fit under 30 % misfit with more than 300 mT·mm³ of strength calls the baseline polluted, prints so, shows it on the LCD, and retakes it up to three times two seconds apart. And while nothing at all is read (strongest smoothed reading under the faint level) the baseline follows the readings with a 5 s time constant, so the 0.1 mT in ten minutes of drift seen on the bench no longer becomes a phantom magnet; a magnet that reads more than the faint level is never absorbed - unless it has been "present" for 30 s without ever fitting: the board's own overnight run, with no magnet in the room, logged 2,293 rough fixes in six hours as drift crossed the 0.04 mT presence line now and then, so something that stays present but never fits as a dipole is taken for drift and followed too (a probe fits; a probe held far away for half a minute would be absorbed, and re-found the moment it fits).

**The driver.** The magnet temperature compensation (MAG_TEMPCO, NdFeB) is off: it scales every reading by 0.12 %/°C of the die's temperature to follow a magnet at the same temperature as the sensor, the probe's magnet is in a hand, and the fit solves the moment every frame anyway, so it only added a die-temperature-tracking gain error. The range stays ±40 mT: the probe magnet at the surface makes 1.6 mT under a sensor and the gains were calibrated at ±40. The CRC is left off because the 1-byte read mode's CRC does not work on this silicon (TI on its forum: 3-byte mode only, at most four data bytes per block; see the study, §2.3); corrupted reads are caught by the median filter and the tracker's gate instead.

**The cursor on the breadboard's LEDs (`src/probeled`).** A table of where every LED sits in breadboard terms (along in rows, across in mm from the centre line) - V6: six holes a side meeting at the centre and two rails a side, 16 × 30; V5: five holes a side across the 7.62 mm channel and the four rails of 25 LEDs as JumperlOS wires them, with the pixel numbering JumperlOS uses (row and hole to `(row-1)*5+column`, the bottom half reversed; which end column 0 is, is an ASSUMPTION with a one-line switch) - and a renderer that puts a bell of light on the cursor: total light held roughly constant so a wider bell is a dimmer one, confidence dims it further, height tints it from white on the board to blue in the air, a far probe is a wide dim purple glow, and in pointed mode a fainter amber mark under the point with a thin tail to the cursor so the two are never confused; per-LED smoothing with a 20 ms attack and 120 ms decay leaves a short comet's tail; gamma 2.2 on the way out. The test bed has no breadboard LEDs, so the LCD's LED screen (`e`) previews it, and `L` streams the cursor as one line per update for a V5: `ports/jumperlos/ProbeCursor` is the JumperlOS module that parses it and paints the same renderer's output into JumperlOS's 10 × 30 graphic overlay (compile-checked against JumperlOS on 2026-09-17, not yet run on a V5; it needs a UART from the array board).

**A V5's LEDs driven from the bench (`src/probeled/LedStrip`, 2026-09-18).** The V5's LED data-in on PD7 (SPI1's MOSI): each WS2812 bit is four SPI bits at 3.125 MHz (1000 / 1100), a 400-LED frame is 4.8 KB and 12.3 ms, sent by DMA1 channel 8 at the highest priority (the LCD's push is channel 3; both run on their own, nothing waits), from a double-buffered frame, every 20 ms tick of the LED service, which now runs at HIGH priority ahead of the display; a frame that would say the same as the last is not sent (a WS2812 keeps what it was given). The looks are levers in the menu's LEDs page: a colour scheme (classic, by height, by confidence, amber, cyan, a running rainbow), full peak (the brightest LED always at the peak, the width alone showing the uncertainty - the default now - or the older constant-total-light rule), bloom, sparkle, pulse, the fade time, a touch ring; the strip brightness lever goes to 1 = the chain's full white. Under it all sits a current budget (menu "max mA", 600 to start, 8 mA per colour at full assumed for the 1010-package LEDs): a frame that would draw more is scaled down whole, and the far-probe glow stays at the dim constant-light rule whatever the peak setting - the first version lit it at 60 % over a hundred LEDs and the V5's 5 V rail browned out (2026-09-18; JumperlOS runs the same LEDs at 10/255).

**Where the choppiness was (2026-09-18).** Kevin saw the LEDs stutter with the display running. Measured with `X`: the LED service was getting ~30 of its 50 ticks a second and sending at half that, because the loop stopped for every display draw, and GFX text was the draw - a size-2 character cost ~200 µs (its fillRect per lit pixel goes through startWrite, writeFastVLine, the virtual drawPixel and the rotation switch each time), so the log screen was 45-60 ms and the 3D scene 7-18 ms; a line cost 3 µs a pixel the same way. `FastDraw.h` writes the same font and the same Bresenham straight into the canvas (5 µs a 100-pixel line against GFX's 297, measured by MagView's boot benchmark): the log screen is now 1.5 ms, the scene 3.6, the LED screen 1.2, and the panel runs at 45 frames a second. MagView also puts a draw off when the LED service is due sooner than the draw would take. Then, with the frames running continuously, the sensor bus started failing: the strip's data line couples into SDA and the I²C block took a glitch for a STOP, dropped out of master mode mid-read, and the Wire library's fixed 10 ms waits then burnt on every flag - up to 190 ms a second lost, 1850 bus resets in 35 s at the worst (and before that, 80 ms holes every few seconds under the old rule that reset the block only once all eight reads had timed out). The sensor read is now its own transaction on the block's registers (`TMAG5273.cpp`): a wait ends the moment the block is no longer master or reports an error, and in 1 ms at most, and a STOP left pending by an aborted read is cleared before the next START. Result: 0 failed reads and 0 resets in 24 s of continuous frames, the sensor pass never over 1.9 ms. The SCK pad (PA5) is switched back to an input after the SPI is set up (the chain only listens to MOSI) and MOSI uses the 10 MHz-class driver, both to give the wiring less to pick up; the wiring itself should still keep the strip's data line away from the sensor wires.

**The on-screen UI (`src/ui`).** The controls V6 will have (an ALPS RKJXM1015004 nav stick - 8-way with a centre push: four direction contacts to a common, a diagonal closes two neighbours, and the push is a contact of its own that also closes on every tilt, so a press is "push held with no direction contact" and a tilt is "a direction contact, whatever the push does", each after 60 ms of settling - the first reading of the drawing had the push closing all four contacts, and a contact trace on 2026-09-18 corrected it; a joystick with a press; two buttons) are read by `Input` (pins in `BoardPins.h`, wired on 2026-09-18; the same events can be typed on the console: arrows, Enter, Tab, backtick, punctuation for the stick) with debounce, repeat and hold events, the joystick as an analog pair and, with hysteresis, a four-way. `tools/hostsim/navtest.cpp` checks the stick decoder with staggered contacts. **The far edge (2026-09-18).** With the probe just at the outer range the glow faded within a second, which looked like autoranging and was two timers: a ROUGH track (rough fixes only, the far glow) was dropped 400 ms after its last rough fix like a real track, while rough fixes at the edge only come at the 200 ms cold-start pace and only while the field is over the presence line, so a hover at the edge flickered off; and the baseline started absorbing a field the moment it dipped under the "nothing here" level, with a 5 s time constant, eating the probe's own field before it came back. **The night of 2026-09-18/19: the probe resting in row 35, hole 3, at 41°.** Kevin left the probe mounted, touching the board at a steep angle, and asked for the tracking, smoothing and sensor fusion to be made as good as they can be and for the main core to be freed. What the static probe showed first (ten minutes of fixes through the fit as it was): the magnet at (1.1, 9.4, 10.8) mm - the array's left edge, 6.7 mm *below* the surface setting, so the tip goes that deep into the hole - fitted with a jitter of 0.09 / 0.16 / 0.12 mm (x / y / z), 1.3° in tilt, strength 4230 ± 100 mT·mm³, misfit 4.5 %, in 1.3 ms a fit; the tracker no steadier than the fixes at rest (its process noise is a hand's), the 1-Euro cursor 0.04 / 0.12 mm. Those are the sensors' numbers (32× on-chip averaging, four reads a frame: the noise ceiling of §2.4 of the prior-art study) and nothing below changed them; what changed is the timing, the latency, the far edge, and what survives a reboot.

*The other core.* The I²C round-robin over the eight sensors - polled, 190 µs a sensor, 65 % of the main loop and a 1.5 ms hole in everything several hundred times a second - now runs on the CH32H417's V3F (`src/magarray/MagSampler`: `loop1()`, the core's `setup1/loop1` convention), reading the I²C block's registers directly and leaving each sensor's bytes in shared RAM under a seqlock; the V5F decodes what is new (the sensor's own SET_COUNT tells a re-read of one conversion from the next), averages into 100 Hz frames as before, and never touches the bus while the sampler runs (recovery, a power-cycle and the bus check pause it). MagArray on the main core went from 1.6 ms a run to 65 µs. Three things the V3F taught: its `micros()` has no sub-millisecond part (the SysTick is the V5F's), so a read timeout on it fails one read in eight - the sampler uses `millis()`; the shared block is NOLOAD and a warm boot found the V3F back on the bus at the old addresses before the V5F had addressed the sensors, so a constructor silences the block before the V3F can look; and under the LED strip's bus glitches the block wedges in a way the read's own checks do not all catch, so the sampler resets the I²C block itself after two passes in which every sensor failed - before that, the main core saw the sensors "lost" and power-cycled and re-addressed them until it stranded them at factory addresses. With that: 0.05 % of reads fail with the strip lit, none are lost. One more thing the V3F taught, in the small hours: after a block reset the sampler ran 158 passes a second where the bus allows ~600 (a read from the main core still took 198 µs, so the bus was fine); the V3F has no instruction cache and fetches flash at what the manual calls a 25 MHz equivalent, so the polling loop saw each flag late and stretched the clock on every byte. The read's hot path (`burstReadOn`, the flag wait, the clock) and `loop1()` are now `__itcm_func` (the V3F reaches the V5F's ITCM in 2 HCLK cycles) with the SDK calls replaced by the register writes they make. Measured: **503 passes a second** with the LED chain streaming, 0 block resets, each 100 Hz frame the average of 3.3 reads of every sensor (it had been 1.6).

*The zero survives a reboot.* Every reflash had re-zeroed with the probe on the board, and the magnet went into the baseline and vanished (the polluted-baseline check cannot see a 0.1 mT magnet among 0.3 mT sensor offsets). The zero as last taken is now saved with the settings and put back at boot; `z` zeroes afresh; and `Y` takes a known magnet back out of a fresh zero - the last good fix (remembered, saved when the probe leaves) or the reference fix measured that night - which is how the clean zero was recovered without touching the probe. Only a fresh zero may be corrected, once.

*The console.* `Serial.write()` waits for every byte: a 150-character row line held the loop 13 ms twice a second, the fix stream 22 ms twenty times a second. The console's transmit is now a 4 KB ring handed to USART1 by DMA (request 85), and the fit rate went from 74 to 99 a second; the frames themselves are on a true 100 Hz grid (they were stamped from the publish, 12-14 ms apart).

*Lag.* `tools/hostsim/pencil.cpp` is a hand that writes (50-250 mm/s strokes, pauses, the pencil turning at up to 1 rad/s) through the real locator and tracker, reporting each output's lag (the delay of the truth it best matches) and the jitter at rest. The tracker's process noise went from 3000 to 10000 mm/s² (lag 20 → 10 ms), the scene's and the cursor's 1-Euro β from 0.05 / 0.1 to 0.5 (40 / 30 ms → 20 ms of lag, 0.025 mm of jitter at rest), and the shaft has a 1-Euro of its own (1 Hz, β 10) in place of a 150 ms time constant: 0.6° at rest, 1.8° while turning at 57°/s. A rough (far) track keeps a small process noise of its own - averaging beats following at 12 mm a fix. The field smoothing's speed rule takes the track's speed through a short low-pass with a 5 mm/s dead band: the raw velocity flickers 10-20 mm/s at rest with the higher process noise and had doubled the fixes' jitter. Settings from the older tuning yield to the new defaults through `SETTINGS_TUNING_VERSION`.

*The smoothing's idea of speed.* The field smoothing backs off with the track's speed, and that speed was the filter's own velocity, which on a probe at rest is the fix jitter over one 10 ms frame: 15 mm/s on the mounted probe (the long log's `track:` lines, x 1.2 +/- 0.1 mm and "speed 15 mm/s" side by side), 50 on one far up. With a fixed 5 mm/s dead band that opened the smoothing on every resting probe, and on a far one it fed on itself - the bench (`pencil.cpp far`, hovering 12-22 mm above the surface) had it at alpha 0.6 and 0.88 mm of frame-to-frame jitter at rest, the smoothing defeated by its own noise. Now the speed is the larger of the distance the track came over the last ten frames and the one-frame velocity, each less 0.4 x what the fix's own error bar makes of it (`MAGLOC_SPEED_JITTER_K`). Bench: far at rest 0.88 to 0.13 mm (the cursor 0.24 to 0.05), far moving 10 to 30 ms of lag; near the board nothing changes (track lag 10 ms, cursor 20 ms), because there the field rule has the smoothing off anyway. Higher K (0.7) buys 0.105 mm at rest for another 10 ms; a moving/at-rest latch with hysteresis was tried and fed on itself the same way. The soak's track error in range went 1.07 to 0.92 mm rms.

*Play.* `src/play`: paint (the point paints the LED it touches, six colours, a brush), target (a green LED to touch; the tally is the time and the miss in mm), and the draw screen on the LCD (the point's path at 3 px/mm over a map of the board). What the tracking looks like to a hand.

*What the datasheets still hold.* Not much on the sampling side now. A TMAG5273 at 32× averaging finishes a three-axis conversion every 2.4 ms, so a 100 Hz frame holds about four independent conversions a sensor, and at 503 passes a second the sampler reads each sensor five times a frame and keeps 3.3 distinct conversions of the four (the SET_COUNT dedup drops the rest): the frame average is at the sensor's own ceiling, and reading faster buys nothing. Less on-chip averaging (16×, 1.2 ms) with more frames averaged on the MCU is the same noise for the same latency, only moved. The 1-byte read mode, the temperature channel off, the dedup and the other core are all in. What is left is not in a register: the noise per fix is set by the sensors' 0.011 mT and their distance from the magnet, so the next factors are the array's geometry (sensors under the far rows, §row-mode notes), the magnet (4200 mT·mm³ here; a stronger or closer one scales the signal one to one) or better parts (TMR, §2 of the prior-art study). On the CH32H417 side the peripherals that mattered are in use (SPI DMA for the LCD bands and the LED chains, USART DMA for the console, the V3F for the bus); the ADC and the hardware FPU are not on the critical path.

Those mirrors are now read from the modules before the apply.

**Settings (`src/settings`, 2026-09-18).** Every menu setting - each NUMBER, TOGGLE and CHOICE item, keyed by page and label - and the row counter's anchors are written as text into the flash tail through the core's EEPROM library (two 8 KB pages used in turn) and put back at boot before the modules run. Saving is by watching: every half second the values are serialised and compared with what was saved, and a change that has stood for two seconds is committed, so a console command's change is saved the same as a menu edit and a lever nudged twenty times is written once. `s` lists what is saved; `Z`, and the menu's "reset settings", put every value back to its compile-time default (snapshotted at boot before the saved values go in), forget the row anchors, and save that. Row mode and the V5 console stream are modes rather than settings and are not saved: the board boots in row mode, quiet. A commit (an 8 KB page erased and written) holds the loop about 70 ms, once per settled change.

### 6.2 Addressing: the address is volatile

All TMAG5273s of one orderable share a factory address (A = 0x35, B = 0x22, C = 0x78, D = 0x44; the digit is the range, x1 = ±40/80 mT, x2 = ±133/266 mT). A new address is written to register 0x0C as `(addr << 1) | 1`.

**That register is reloaded from the factory value at every power-up.** The datasheet says so twice: "at each power cycle these bits must be written again to avoid going back to default factory address", and under the multi-sensor procedure, "Repeat the above steps if there is a power outage or power-up reset condition." There is no way to store an address in the part.

The consequence for the plan of record: *the per-sensor power pins cannot be given back after a one-time setup and reused as interrupt lines.* The power-up walk has to run at every boot, so each sensor needs a switchable supply for good. Ways to live with that on a real board:

- INT is open-drain and can be shared by every sensor on one line, so interrupts cost one pin, not eight. The firmware then has to poll to see who fired, which at 100 Hz polling it is doing anyway.
- Mix the four orderables (A/B/C/D): four sensors share a bus with no address work at all, and a VCC switch is needed only per group of same-letter parts.
- A VCC-column × ground-row matrix (the Jerorack schematic's MVCC/MGND labels) powers N×M sensors from N+M pins, with a caution: a sensor whose ground is floating while its VCC is high can be half-powered through its I²C pins when the bus goes low, and may answer at the factory address. Drive unused grounds *high* rather than floating them, and verify on hardware.
- A shift register or I/O expander for the supplies if pins are short; sensors draw ~2–3 mA each.

A fixed-address part (the MMC56x3 at 0x30) takes no part in any of this: it is powered for good, one per bus, and simply looked for at its address once the walk is done (`beginFixedSensor`).

What the firmware does at every boot: ground pins low; all VCC pins **driven low** (not floated: a floating supply can be back-powered through the bus pull-ups and the ESD structure) for 100 ms so the bypass capacitors empty; then for each sensor, VCC high, wait 5 ms (270 µs is the part's own power-up time), probe all four factory addresses, move it to 0x10 + i, check TI's manufacturer ID (0x5449) at the new address, configure. A sensor that cannot be addressed has its VCC put back low for the rest of the walk, so it cannot answer for the next one.

Power is multiplexed only during that walk. When it ends every sensor is powered and stays powered, including any that did not answer. A sensor that later stops answering is first looked for at its own assigned address without touching anyone's supply: a bus hiccup does not reset a sensor, so it is normally still there and only needs configuring again. Supplies are switched again only if something turns up back at a factory address, which means a sensor really did reset; then the lost sensors, and only those, are walked again while the rest keep running.

### 6.3 Sensor configuration

Continuous conversion of X, Y, Z and die temperature; low-noise mode; 32× averaging (400 results/s, the quietest setting); no magnet temperature compensation (it was NdFeB until 2026-09-18; see "The driver" above); the narrow range (±40 mT on an x1 part), with saturated sensors dropped from the fit. One read of 9 bytes from 0x10 returns temperature, X, Y, Z and the conversion status (the temperature channel is on so the compensation certainly has a temperature to use, and so per-sensor drift can be watched; the `m` table shows it). At 400 kHz the whole array takes ~2.5 ms, and the firmware reads it at 100 Hz.

Datasheet noise at 25 °C (RMS): X and Y 22 µT at 32× averaging and 110–125 µT at 1×; Z 9–11 µT at 32× and 66–68 µT at 1×. Offset is ±300 µT typical and drifts ±3 µT/°C, which is what the baseline subtraction removes.

The MMC56x3s (`MAG_MMC_*`): continuous mode at 150 Hz, the 3.5 ms decimation filter (2 mG = 0.2 µT rms a result), Auto_SR_en (a SET/RESET around every measurement, which the datasheet's figures assume), no temperature channel (it needs a one-shot). ±3 mT full scale on each axis, 0.0625 mG a count.

### 6.4 The fit

A magnet far enough away is a point dipole with moment **m** at **p0**; at a sensor at **p**, with **r** = **p** − **p0**:

```
B = 3 r (m·r) / |r|^5  −  m / |r|^3
```

Units are chosen so there are no constants: mm, mT, and the moment in mT·mm³ (μ0 m / 4π; a 6×3 mm N52 disc is ≈ 9800, a 4×2 mm ≈ 2900). Six unknowns, three equations per sensor.

The field is *linear* in **m**, so for any trial position the best moment is one 3×3 linear solve (variable projection), and only the three position coordinates are searched, by Levenberg–Marquardt with a numeric Jacobian. That keeps millimetre-sized and 10⁴-sized unknowns out of one matrix and needs no starting guess for the moment. Cold start seeds over the strongest readings at three heights plus a ring of four low seeds (a magnet close to the board beside a sensor has a false minimum pinned under that sensor); while tracking, the last fix is the seed and a fix takes two or three iterations. With 3-axis sensors in a plane there is no above/below mirror ambiguity. A fix is called valid when the RMS residual is under 25 % of the RMS signal. Dead sensors are left out of the fit.

Outputs: magnet position, the unit axis of the magnet (S→N), tilt from the board normal, |m| (constant for one magnet, so a free sanity check), residual. The probe tip is magnet position plus a fixed offset along the axis (`MAGLOC_TIP_OFFSET_MM`, zero until there is a probe to measure).

### 6.5 What simulation says about accuracy

Host-side tests (`pio test -e native`) generate the field of a known dipole at the 4×2, 20 mm pitch array and fit it back.

- With perfect readings the solver is exact from a cold start: 49,000 positions over and 10 mm beyond the array, heights 3–45 mm, tilts 0–60°, worst error under 0.0001 mm.
- With Gaussian noise at the datasheet level for 16× averaging (31 µT on X and Y, 13 µT on Z), one frame, magnet over the array's footprint:

| Height above the sensors | 6×3 mm N52, RMS error | worst | 4×2 mm N52, RMS error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 mm | 0.04 mm | 0.15 mm | 0.16 mm |
| 10 mm | 0.10 mm | 0.28 mm | ~0.35 mm |
| 15 mm | 0.25 mm | 0.70 mm | ~1.0 mm |
| 20 mm | 0.63 mm | 1.7 mm | ~2.2 mm |
| 25 mm | 1.3 mm | 3.7 mm | ~5 mm |
| 30 mm | 2.5 mm | 6.3 mm | no fix |

Ten millimetres outside the footprint the error is roughly 0.4 mm RMS up to 10 mm height and grows from there. Over the footprint the error grows as roughly the cube of height (the field gradient falls as 1/r⁴, softened by more sensors sharing the signal), so every mm of stack-up between sensors and breadboard face counts; a breadboard is ~8.5 mm thick, which puts a tip at the surface already at ~10 mm plus however far up the probe the magnet sits. Averaging N frames divides these by √N (100 Hz frames, 10 Hz output: 3× better).

**This is the noise floor, not the error budget.** Still to come on real hardware: sensor placement and rotation errors (1 mm of placement error is about 1 mm of answer), sensitivity error (±5 %-class per axis, calibratable), the dipole approximation for a magnet only a few diameters away, offset drift with temperature, and anything steel on the breadboard. The realistic expectation is a few tenths of a mm close in after calibration, against the ~0.3 mm estimated in the original chat.

### 6.6 First-run checklist

1. Fit pull-ups on SDA/SCL. Flash (`pio run -t upload`), then open the console at 115200 on the WCH-Link's serial port. If another debug probe is plugged into the same computer (a Raspberry Pi Debug Probe was, when this was written), name the port: `pio device monitor -p /dev/cu.usbmodemXXXX`, or set `monitor_port` in `platformio.ini`. If nothing prints at all, the first suspects are solder bridges SB3/SB4 next to the WCH-LinkE (they carry the UART to PA10/PA9); the green LED blinking at 1 Hz means the firmware itself is running.
2. If no sensor answers at all, run `b` (bus check). It gives a verdict for SCL and SDA (ok / not pulled up / held low), says whether powering any one sensor changes that, then powers each sensor alone and lists what acknowledges, and finally tries the spare header pins as the supply for any sensor that stayed silent. Pull-ups are 2.2k–4.7k from PA8 and from PC9 to 3.3 V (J6 pin 1 is 3V3).
3. Boot prints the array table. "no answer" on sensor 3 means the PA14 guess is wrong: fix `PIN_MAG_VCC_3` in `src/board/BoardPins.h`. The `part` column tells which orderable is fitted.
4. `i` (identify), then hold the magnet over each sensor in turn. The strongest-sensor index should follow the table in `src/magarray/MagArrayConfig.h`; fix the order there if not.
5. `o` (orientation check) does most of the next step for you. Hold a magnet 1–2 cm over the array, off-centre, and press `o`: it fits the magnet under all 256 combinations of rows swapped or not, each row's order along x reversed or not, top or underside, and each row's package rotation, and lists the best (about 15 s). The true mounting fits to a few percent and everything else to tens of percent; repeat at two or three positions. Every answer has a mirror twin (rows swapped, other board side, both rotations turned 180°) that fits equally well, because the mirror image of a magnet and sensors is also magnetically valid, so which row is which (or which side the parts are on) has to come from looking at the board. On this bench sensors 0–3 are the y = pitch row and 4–7 the y = 0 row.
6. With the magnet's north pole facing down over a sensor, that sensor's Bz should be negative (field lines leave a north pole, so under it they point down into the board, and board +z is up), and as the magnet slides along +x across the sensor, Bx should go from positive to negative. Wrong signs mean `rotationDeg` or `underside` for that sensor. The TMAG5273's own sign convention is the other way up (positive Z is field going *into* the package top, datasheet figure 6-1); the firmware converts, and the table only describes how each package is mounted.
7. Measure the real sensor pitch and put it in `MAG_PITCH_X_MM` / `MAG_PITCH_Y_MM`.
8. `z` re-zeroes the baseline with the magnet away. `l` prints the fix, `d` streams fixes as CSV, `f` streams raw fields, `v` cycles the camera, `X` shows service timing.
9. Watch the fit time (`l`, or the bottom line of the LCD). A tracking fix is two or three iterations and should take a few hundred microseconds at most (not yet measured on the chip); if it sits in the milliseconds, every frame is failing and cold-starting (seven seeds, up to 30 iterations each), which is what a wrong geometry or orientation table looks like.
10. Check |m| stays constant as the magnet moves. If it changes with height the geometry table is off; if the residual is large the orientation table is.

Experiments worth running once it tracks: a ruler sweep in top view for linearity and scale; repeatability at one spot (noise against the table above); height sweep; tilt sweep; then a breadboard on top with steel-legged parts and jumper wires, and a power resistor or relay nearby for ambient rejection; then magnet size against usable height.

## 7. CH32H417 toolchain notes

- **Upload with wlink, not OpenOCD.** With `upload_protocol = wch-link` OpenOCD writes and verifies the image, then fails on the reset (`Unsupported register`, `Failed to write memory … 0x20000000`, exit −11) and leaves the chip halted. `upload_protocol = wlink` (wlink 0.1.2, which the platform installs) flashes the ELF and resets cleanly. The Arduino core's own hardware tests use wlink and warn that OpenOCD erases only the first 448 KB of this part, so the two should not be mixed. The WCH-LinkE has its own USB-C port; the chip's USB ports do not flash it.
- **PlatformIO works.** `Community-PIO-CH32V/platform-ch32v` has CH32H415/416/417 board definitions (generic ones as `_V3F` / `_V5F` pairs for the two cores), with `noneos-sdk` and `ch32v003fun` frameworks and upload by `wch-link`, `minichlink`, `isp` or `wlink`. Its README does not mention the H4 family yet; the board files do.
- **There is an Arduino core**, `Community-PIO-CH32V/ArduinoCore-CH32H4`, merged into the platform on 2026-09-13. It is modelled on arduino-pico, which is what JumperlOS runs on: `setup()`/`loop()` on the 400 MHz V5F, `setup1()`/`loop1()` on the 100 MHz V3F, one ELF for both cores, USB CDC `Serial` through a TinyUSB fork, Wire and SPI with `setSCL`/`setSDA`/`setSCK`-style pin selection that resolves the peripheral from the pins, DMA for SPI blocks over 32 bytes, LittleFS, SD, Ethernet with lwIP and TLS, ~700 KB of heap, and a 382-test suite of which 273 run on hardware. It sets VIO18 to 3.3 V and enables the V5F instruction cache (worth a measured 145×). Board ID `ch32h417qeu6_evt_r0` (WCH's EVT board; the nano carries the same QEU6, only the wiring differs). This is what Testolomew uses, pinned to platform commit `8cf3b51`.
- **`maxgerhardt/ch32h417lib`** is WCH's peripheral library (EVT V1.5, unmodified) packaged as a submodule for the MicroPython CH32 port; the Arduino core carries the same library under `system/`. **`openwch/ch32h417`** is WCH's own EVT repository with the examples it came from.
- **ch32fun** has early CH32H41x support: register header `ch32h41xhw.h`, five examples (blink, UART, debug printf, dual-core hello), minichlink support. No I²C or SPI helpers for the family and nothing sets VIO18. Fine for a bare-metal experiment (add an env with `framework = ch32v003fun` and board `genericCH32H417QEU6_V5F`); not the base for something meant to share code with JumperlOS.
- **The nano board** (from its schematic): onboard WCH-LinkE (a CH32V305) for flashing, debugging (SWD on PB8/PB9) and a serial port on USART1 (PA9/PA10), all through solder bridges SB3–SB6; LEDs on PC3 (blue) and PC2 (green), active low; 12-pin FPC LCD on SPI2 (SCK PB13, MOSI PB15, CS PB12, DC PD9, RST PD8, backlight always on, no MISO), which wuxx's demo drives as a 1.54" 240×240 ST7789 in SPI mode 3 at 36 MHz; SD card on SDMMC; 25 MHz crystal. Headers J5/J6/J9/J10 carry the GPIOs.

## 8. The Testolomew firmware in brief

PlatformIO, Arduino framework, laid out like JumperlOS (feature folders under `src/`, bare `#include "Foo.h"`, the same `src_subdirs_include.py`, `.clang-format` and `.clangd`). `src/jos/JumperlOS.h` is a cut-down copy of JumperlOS's `Service` / `jOSmanager` with the same names and semantics, so a module written here registers with `jOS` exactly as it would there. Modules: `rowcount` (breadboard row from the fix, the hold-still test), `board` (pins, VIO check), `console` (single-character commands that modules register themselves), `magarray` (TMAG5273 driver and the array service), `magfit` (the pure-math fit, host-testable, and the locator service), `display` (ST7789 push and the 3D view). `src/config.h` switches modules on and off. See the repository README for how to add one.

The LCD frame (115 KB) was at first sent 30 rows per scheduler tick, so the array keeps its 100 Hz; one band measured 4.8 ms (more than the SPI clock alone would give - the clock was 25 MHz, not the 36 asked for), for about 18 frames per second, later 10-13 with more on the screen. Since 2026-09-18 the frame goes in two DMA bands that run while the loop reads sensors, into a second framebuffer while the first is on the wire, at 50 MHz: 36 frames per second (see §6.1, "The on-screen UI").

## 9. Recommendation

Build both prototypes; they answer different questions and share nothing but the probe body.

1. **Magnetics on Testolomew now**, since the hardware is on the bench. The question it answers is whether real-world errors (steel parts, placement, near-field) leave the few-tenths-of-a-mm that the noise floor promises, and how high above a real breadboard stack-up a probe-sized magnet can be tracked.
2. **Ratio ladder on V5 next**, from the existing spec, Scheme C first. The question it answers is whether ratio invariance holds with a real hand and a real user circuit.
3. If V6 ends up with an LED under every hole, add the **a–e LED code** to either; it is a phototransistor and firmware.
4. Keep a **galvanic contact channel** whatever else is chosen.
5. If DC magnetics works but drift or clippings are a nuisance, the **AC coil** version reuses the geometry and the fit.

## 10. Open questions

- Which pin really powers sensor 3, and the array's true pitch, order and orientation (§6.6).
- The probe's magnet: size against usable height (table in §6.5), and how far up the barrel it can sit before tilt error dominates the tip position.
- Whether steel-legged parts and jumper wires on the breadboard shift the fit by more than the hole tolerance.
- How many sensors a full V6 board needs at what pitch. Noise-limited error grows as roughly the cube of distance, so pitch is set by the stack-up height.
- How V6 powers and addresses ~12–24 TMAG5273s (§6.2), and whether mixing orderables is acceptable for purchasing.
- For the ladder: ladder total resistance against carrier frequency, the guard-strip layout, and the analog switch that lets contact mode coexist.
- Whether the second RP2350 mentioned at the start is still in the architecture now that the CH32H417 is the coprocessor, and which chip owns probe sensing.

## 11. Corrections to the original chat

- "You already have firmware" (for the Jerorack magnetometer array): there is none; the Jerorack repository is KiCad only. The TMAG5273 driver here is new.
- "Four default I²C addresses per variant, four variants" and the idea that addresses can be set once: there are four factory addresses (one per orderable letter) and an assigned address does not survive a power cycle (§6.2).
- "~0.3 mm" for the magnet approach was a guess. Simulation puts the noise floor at 0.04–0.6 mm between 5 and 20 mm of height for a 6×3 mm N52 on a 20 mm grid, with calibration errors on top (§6.5).
- "Fit … in well under a millisecond, ~500 Hz updates": the fit is cheap, but eight sensors on one 400 kHz I²C bus take ~2.5 ms per frame, so ~300 Hz is the ceiling for one bus, and the firmware runs at 100 Hz. More buses or 1 MHz I²C raise it.
- The chat assumed a second RP2350 would do this work; the design has since moved to a CH32H417 coprocessor, which is what the proof of concept runs on.
